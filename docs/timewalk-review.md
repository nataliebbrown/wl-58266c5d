# TimeWalk — content for review

TimeWalk includes three kinds of written content:

1. **Dating caveats** — short notes wherever scholars disagree on dates. These are **live now** (they make the dates more honest). Edit them in `src/lib/timewalk/eras.ts` (`CAVEATS`).
2. **Archaeology finds** — **drafts, hidden until reviewed.** To publish one, set `reviewed: true` in `src/lib/timewalk/archaeology.ts`.
3. **Daily-life topics** — **drafts, hidden until reviewed.** To publish one, set `reviewed: true` in `src/lib/timewalk/dailyLife.ts`.

**To preview drafts in the app**, open any page with `?drafts=1` at the end of the address (e.g. `http://localhost:8080/timewalk?drafts=1`). Drafts then show with a “Draft” label until you turn preview off with `?drafts=0`.

## 1. Dating caveats (live)

### Dating Genesis 1–11

WL does not put scholarly dates on Genesis 1–11. Christians hold different views on the age of the earth and on how to read these chapters — as straightforward chronology, as true history told with gaps in the genealogies, or as a theological account with a literary structure. The traditional dates (Creation in 4004/4003 BC, the Flood in 2348 BC) come from Archbishop James Ussher, who added up the genealogies in 1650. Many Christians today do not hold to those dates, and many do.

### When did Abraham live?

Dates for Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, and Joseph are approximate. They depend on the date of the Exodus and on how the 430 years of Exodus 12:40 are counted (the Greek Old Testament and Galatians 3:17 count them from Abraham, not from Jacob’s move to Egypt). Most scholars place the patriarchs somewhere around 2100–1800 BC.

### Early or late Exodus?

Evangelical scholars hold two main dates. The early date, about 1446 BC, comes from 1 Kings 6:1, which says Solomon began the temple 480 years after the Exodus (around 966 BC). The late date, about 1260 BC, comes from Exodus 1:11 (the store city of Rameses) and from how some read the archaeology of Canaan. Some take the 480 years as a symbolic number (twelve generations of forty). Both views are held by people who trust the Bible.

### The judges probably overlapped

If you add up every judge’s years in sequence, the total is longer than the time available. Most scholars think several judges led different regions at the same time, so the period is shorter than the sum — roughly 1380–1050 BC on the early Exodus date, or 1200–1050 BC on the late date.

### Dates of the kings

Kings and Chronicles give reign lengths for every king, but working them into exact years is complicated by co-regencies (a son ruling alongside his father) and different ways of counting a king’s first year. Scholars differ by a few years (Edwin Thiele’s chronology is widely used). Ussher’s traditional dates for this period run about 40 years earlier than most scholars’ today.

### 587 or 586 BC?

Jerusalem fell to Babylon in either 587 or 586 BC. The difference comes from how the years of the Judean and Babylonian kings are counted. Both dates appear in reputable sources.

### When did Job live?

Job’s story feels like the time of the patriarchs — wealth counted in flocks, a father offering sacrifices for his family, very long life — so it is often placed there. But the book does not say when Job lived, and its date of writing is unknown.

### The Psalms span centuries

The Psalms were written over about a thousand years, from Moses (Psalm 90) to after the exile (Psalm 137). They are grouped with David here because he wrote about half of them.

### When was Daniel written?

Daniel is set in Babylon and Persia in the 6th century BC, and conservative scholars date the book there. Many critical scholars date its writing to the 2nd century BC, around the Maccabean revolt, partly because its prophecies describe that period so precisely. WL follows the book’s own setting.

### Who was Ahasuerus?

Most scholars identify King Ahasuerus in Esther with Xerxes I of Persia (486–465 BC), which places Esther around 483–473 BC. Ussher’s traditional chronology identified him with a different king, so its dates for Esther are earlier.

### When did Ezra arrive?

Ezra 7 says Ezra came to Jerusalem in “the seventh year of King Artaxerxes.” Most scholars take this as Artaxerxes I, giving 458 BC (before Nehemiah in 445 BC). Some argue for Artaxerxes II, which would be 398 BC.

### Why “BC” for Jesus’ birth?

Herod the Great died in about 4 BC, and Jesus was born while Herod was alive (Matthew 2), so Jesus was likely born around 6–4 BC. The calendar that counts years from Jesus’ birth was worked out in AD 525, and its starting point was off by a few years.

### AD 30 or AD 33?

Most scholars date the crucifixion to Passover in either AD 30 or AD 33. Both fit the years Pontius Pilate governed Judea (AD 26–36) and the calendar of that Passover. The difference depends partly on whether Jesus’ ministry lasted about two or three years.

### Dates of the letters

The New Testament letters can usually be dated only approximately, from details in the letters and in Acts. Scholars differ by a few years for most of them.

### When was Revelation written?

Most scholars date Revelation to about AD 95, during the reign of the emperor Domitian, as early church writers said. Some argue for the late AD 60s, under Nero, before the temple was destroyed in AD 70.

## 2. Archaeology finds (drafts)

### Merneptah Stele

*c. 1208 BC · Found 1896, Thebes, Egypt · Today: Egyptian Museum, Cairo*

A granite monument celebrating Pharaoh Merneptah’s military campaigns, including one into Canaan.

It contains the earliest known mention of “Israel” outside the Bible — as a people already living in Canaan by about 1208 BC. It fits the Bible’s picture of Israel settled in the land during the time of the judges.

### Tel Dan Stele

*c. 840 BC · Found 1993–1994, Tel Dan, northern Israel · Today: Israel Museum, Jerusalem*

Fragments of a victory inscription by an Aramean king, probably Hazael of Damascus.

It mentions the “house of David” — the earliest reference to King David outside the Bible, written about 150 years after his reign. It also refers to kings of Israel and Judah around the time described in 2 Kings 8–9.

### Mesha Stele (Moabite Stone)

*c. 840 BC · Found 1868, Dhiban, Jordan · Today: Louvre, Paris*

A basalt monument by Mesha, king of Moab, describing his revolt against Israel.

Mesha names Omri, king of Israel, and the God of Israel (YHWH). 2 Kings 3 tells the same story of Mesha’s rebellion from Israel’s side. Many scholars also read “house of David” in a damaged line.

### Black Obelisk of Shalmaneser III

*c. 825 BC · Found 1846, Nimrud, Iraq · Today: British Museum, London*

A black limestone pillar recording the Assyrian king’s campaigns, with carved panels of kings bringing tribute.

One panel shows “Jehu, son of Omri” (as the Assyrians called him) bowing before Shalmaneser — the only known picture of an Israelite king. Jehu’s rise is told in 2 Kings 9–10.

### Hezekiah’s Tunnel and the Siloam Inscription

*c. 701 BC · Found 1880, Jerusalem · Today: Tunnel in Jerusalem; inscription in the Istanbul Archaeology Museums*

A 533-meter (1,750-foot) tunnel cut through solid rock to bring water from the Gihon Spring into the city, with an inscription describing the moment the two digging teams met.

The Bible says Hezekiah “made the pool and the tunnel and brought water into the city” to prepare for the Assyrian siege. Visitors can still walk through it today.

### Sennacherib’s Prism

*c. 690 BC · Found 1830, Nineveh, Iraq · Today: British Museum (Taylor Prism); Oriental Institute, Chicago*

Six-sided clay prisms recording the Assyrian king Sennacherib’s campaigns.

Sennacherib boasts that he shut up Hezekiah in Jerusalem “like a bird in a cage” — but never claims to have captured the city. 2 Kings 19 and Isaiah 37 tell how God delivered Jerusalem.

### Lachish Reliefs

*c. 700 BC · Found 1840s, Sennacherib’s palace at Nineveh · Today: British Museum, London*

Large carved stone panels from Sennacherib’s palace showing the siege and capture of the Judean city of Lachish.

The Bible mentions Sennacherib at Lachish while he threatened Jerusalem. The reliefs show siege ramps, battering rams, and families being led into exile — and excavations at Lachish have found the Assyrian siege ramp.

### Seal impression of King Hezekiah

*c. 715–686 BC · Found 2009 (published 2015), Ophel excavations, Jerusalem · Today: Herbert W. Armstrong College / Hebrew University collection*

A small clay seal impression (bulla) reading “Belonging to Hezekiah, son of Ahaz, king of Judah.”

It was found in a scientific excavation near the royal quarter of Jerusalem and names the king described in 2 Kings 18–20.

### Ketef Hinnom Silver Scrolls

*c. 600 BC · Found 1979, a burial cave in Jerusalem · Today: Israel Museum, Jerusalem*

Two tiny rolled-up silver amulets, worn around the neck.

They contain a form of the priestly blessing from Numbers 6:24–26 — “The LORD bless you and keep you…” — making them the oldest known texts of Scripture, about 400 years older than the Dead Sea Scrolls.

### Lachish Letters

*c. 588 BC · Found 1935, Lachish · Today: Israel Museum and British Museum*

Letters written in ink on pottery pieces by a Judean officer, as Babylon closed in.

One letter says the writer can no longer see the fire signals of Azekah. Jeremiah 34:7 describes the moment when Lachish and Azekah were the only fortified cities of Judah left standing.

### Babylonian Chronicle of Jerusalem’s capture

*c. 597 BC (written later) · Found Acquired by the British Museum in the 1890s · Today: British Museum, London*

A clay tablet recording the early years of Nebuchadnezzar’s reign.

It records that Nebuchadnezzar captured “the city of Judah” on 2 Adar (March 16, 597 BC), took its king, and appointed a king of his own choosing — the events of 2 Kings 24:10–17.

### Jehoiachin ration tablets

*c. 592–569 BC · Found 1899–1917, Babylon · Today: Vorderasiatisches Museum, Berlin*

Administrative tablets listing food rations given to people held at Nebuchadnezzar’s court.

They list rations for “Jehoiachin, king of Judah” and his sons — matching 2 Kings 25:27–30, which says the exiled king was given a regular allowance in Babylon.

### Ishtar Gate of Babylon

*c. 575 BC · Found 1902–1914, Babylon · Today: Reconstructed in the Pergamon Museum, Berlin*

A glazed-brick city gate decorated with lions, bulls, and dragons, built by Nebuchadnezzar II.

It gives a sense of the city Daniel and the exiles lived in — and of the pride in Nebuchadnezzar’s words, “Is not this great Babylon, which I have built?”

### Nabonidus Cylinder

*c. 540 BC · Found 1854, Ur, Iraq · Today: British Museum, London*

Clay cylinders recording the building work of Nabonidus, the last king of Babylon.

They name Belshazzar as Nabonidus’ eldest son. Belshazzar was once unknown outside the Bible; Daniel 5 presents him ruling Babylon, which fits his role as co-regent while his father was away — and explains why he offered Daniel only the “third” place in the kingdom.

### Cyrus Cylinder

*c. 539 BC · Found 1879, Babylon · Today: British Museum, London*

A clay cylinder in which Cyrus the Great describes his capture of Babylon.

Cyrus says he returned displaced peoples to their homelands and restored their temples. It does not mention the Jews, but it shows the policy behind his decree in Ezra 1 — and Isaiah names Cyrus as the one who would say of Jerusalem, “Let it be rebuilt.”

### Dead Sea Scrolls (Great Isaiah Scroll)

*c. 250 BC – AD 68 (Isaiah scroll c. 125 BC) · Found 1947–1956, caves near Qumran · Today: Shrine of the Book, Israel Museum, Jerusalem*

Around 900 scrolls, including copies or fragments of every Old Testament book except Esther.

The complete Isaiah scroll is about 1,000 years older than the previous oldest Hebrew manuscripts, yet it reads almost word for word the same — powerful evidence of how carefully Scripture was copied.

### Pool of Bethesda

*1st century and earlier · Found Excavated from the late 1800s, near St. Anne’s Church, Jerusalem · Today: Jerusalem (visitable)*

The remains of large pools with porches, north of the temple.

John 5:2 describes a pool by the Sheep Gate “surrounded by five covered colonnades.” The excavations revealed twin pools with porticoes on four sides and one between them — five in all.

### Pool of Siloam

*1st century · Found 2004, City of David, Jerusalem · Today: Jerusalem (visitable)*

Wide stone steps of a large first-century pool at the southern end of the City of David, fed by Hezekiah’s Tunnel.

Jesus sent a man born blind to “wash in the Pool of Siloam” (John 9:7). The pool was rediscovered by workers repairing a sewer line.

### The Galilee Boat

*c. 50 BC – AD 50 · Found 1986, shore of the Sea of Galilee · Today: Yigal Allon Centre, Kibbutz Ginosar*

A wooden fishing boat about 8 meters (27 feet) long, preserved in mud and revealed during a drought.

It is the kind of boat the disciples would have used — large enough for a crew of a dozen or more — and helps picture the storm on the lake and the great catch of fish.

### Capernaum synagogue

*4th–5th century, built on a 1st-century foundation · Found Excavated from the early 1900s, Capernaum · Today: Capernaum, Sea of Galilee (visitable)*

The ruins of a white limestone synagogue, built over the black basalt foundations of an earlier one.

Many archaeologists think the basalt foundations belong to the synagogue where Jesus taught and healed in Capernaum, His base in Galilee.

### Pilate Stone

*c. AD 26–36 · Found 1961, theater of Caesarea Maritima · Today: Israel Museum, Jerusalem (replica at Caesarea)*

A limestone block with a Latin dedication of a building to the emperor Tiberius.

It names “Pontius Pilate, Prefect of Judea” — the governor who sentenced Jesus — and confirms his official title.

### Caiaphas Ossuary

*1st century AD · Found 1990, a burial cave in Jerusalem · Today: Israel Museum, Jerusalem*

An ornate limestone bone box inscribed “Joseph, son of Caiaphas.”

Most scholars believe it belonged to the high priest Caiaphas (Josephus gives his full name as Joseph Caiaphas), who presided at Jesus’ trial.

### Temple warning inscription

*1st century BC – AD 70 · Found 1871 and 1935, Jerusalem · Today: Istanbul Archaeology Museums; Israel Museum (fragment)*

A stone sign in Greek from the barrier around Herod’s temple courts.

It warns that any foreigner who goes beyond the barrier will have only himself to blame for his death. Paul was accused of bringing a Gentile past it (Acts 21:28), and he wrote that Christ “has broken down the dividing wall of hostility” (Ephesians 2:14).

### Gallio Inscription (Delphi)

*c. AD 52 · Found Published 1905, Delphi, Greece · Today: Delphi Archaeological Museum*

Fragments of a letter from the emperor Claudius that mentions Gallio as proconsul of Achaia.

It dates Gallio’s term to about AD 51–52 — which dates Paul’s appearance before him in Corinth (Acts 18:12) and anchors much of the timeline of Paul’s journeys.

### Erastus Inscription (Corinth)

*c. AD 50 · Found 1929, Corinth, Greece · Today: Corinth (in place)*

A Latin pavement inscription saying a man named Erastus laid the pavement at his own expense in return for his office.

Paul sends greetings from “Erastus, the city treasurer” of Corinth (Romans 16:23). Many scholars think this is the same man, though others note the name was common and the inscription’s date is debated.

### Arch of Titus

*c. AD 81 · Found Standing in the Roman Forum since antiquity · Today: Rome*

A triumphal arch celebrating the Roman victory over Judea in AD 70.

A carved panel shows soldiers carrying the menorah and other treasures from the destroyed temple — the fulfillment of Jesus’ words that “not one stone will be left on another.”

## 3. Daily-life topics (drafts)

### Shepherds and sheep

Sheep and goats were the wealth of many families. Shepherds led their flocks — walking in front, not driving from behind — to grass and water, often over long distances. They knew each animal, guarded them at night in stone folds, and fought off lions, bears, and thieves. It was humble, dangerous work; shepherds were often young, poor, or looked down on.

**What it shows about God:** God calls Himself the Shepherd of His people, and Jesus is the Good Shepherd who knows His sheep by name and lays down His life for them.

### The farming year

Life followed the rains. The early rains (October–November) softened the ground for plowing and sowing; the late rains (March–April) ripened the crops. Barley was harvested in spring, wheat in early summer, then grapes, figs, and olives in late summer and fall. A failed rain meant hunger, so harvest was a time of joy — and the law told farmers to leave the edges of their fields for the poor.

**What it shows about God:** Every harvest was a gift from the God who sends rain, and the gleaning laws show His care for the poor and the foreigner.

### Bread and meals

Bread was the center of every meal — usually barley or wheat, ground by hand each morning and baked in flat loaves. With it came olive oil, lentils, cheese, fruit, and occasionally fish; meat was mostly for feasts. Eating together created a bond, which is why sharing a table with someone was a sign of acceptance, and why it mattered so much who Jesus ate with.

**What it shows about God:** God fed Israel with manna in the wilderness, and Jesus calls Himself the Bread of Life — then welcomes sinners to His table.

### Water and wells

In a dry land, water meant life. Families depended on springs, cisterns cut into rock to catch winter rain, and deep wells — which were often places of meeting (and of conflict). Drawing water was daily work, usually done by women in the cool of morning or evening. A spring that flowed all year was called “living water.”

**What it shows about God:** God provided water from the rock in the wilderness, and Jesus offers “living water” that satisfies forever.

### Houses and homes

Most families lived in small houses of stone or mud brick around a shared courtyard, often with relatives next door. Animals might be kept in the lower part of the house at night. A flat roof, reached by a ladder or stairs, was used for drying crops, sleeping in summer, and praying. Homes were dim, crowded, and shared — privacy was rare, and hospitality was expected.

**What it shows about God:** Friends carried a paralyzed man up onto such a roof and lowered him to Jesus — who forgave his sins and healed him.

### Family, inheritance, and the firstborn

Family meant the extended household, not just parents and children, and land was passed down within the family so that its name would continue. The firstborn son usually received a double share of the inheritance and responsibility for the family. Keeping land in the family was so important that relatives were expected to buy it back if it had to be sold.

**What it shows about God:** Again and again God overturns expectations — choosing younger sons like Jacob, Joseph, and David — to show that His grace, not birth order, decides.

### Betrothal and weddings

Marriages were usually arranged between families. Betrothal was a binding commitment — ending it required a divorce — and could last a year while the groom prepared a home, often by adding a room to his father’s house. Then the groom came, often at night, to bring his bride home with a procession and a feast that could last a week.

**What it shows about God:** Scripture pictures God as a faithful husband to His people, and Jesus as the Bridegroom who goes to prepare a place and will come back for His bride.

### Hospitality

Welcoming strangers was a sacred duty, especially for travelers who had no inns to rely on. A host offered water to wash dusty feet, food, and shelter, and was responsible for the guest’s safety. Failing to show hospitality was shameful.

**What it shows about God:** Abraham welcomed three visitors and found the LORD among them. God, who welcomed us when we were strangers, calls His people to welcome others.

### Honor and shame

In the ancient Mediterranean world, a person’s honor — their standing in the eyes of the community — mattered enormously. Seating at meals, public praise, and family reputation all showed honor. Being publicly humiliated was one of the worst things that could happen.

**What it shows about God:** Jesus took the place of deepest shame on a cross, “despising the shame,” and God has highly exalted Him — and He gives honor to those the world overlooks.

### Travel and roads

Most people walked — about 20 miles (30 km) on a good day. Donkeys carried goods; camels crossed deserts; wealthier people might ride. In New Testament times, Roman roads and relative peace made travel safer, and ships carried passengers between ports in the sailing season (roughly spring to fall). Paul traveled thousands of miles this way.

**What it shows about God:** God used Roman roads and ships to carry the good news across the empire, exactly as Jesus said it would spread “to the ends of the earth.”

### Fishing on the Sea of Galilee

The Sea of Galilee is a freshwater lake about 13 miles (21 km) long, known for sudden storms. Fishermen like Peter, Andrew, James, and John worked in family businesses with hired help, often at night, using large dragnets and cast nets from wooden boats. Fish were salted and dried and sold across the region.

**What it shows about God:** Jesus called fishermen to follow Him and promised to make them “fishers of people” — and He showed His power over the sea that frightened them.

### Money, taxes, and tax collectors

Under Rome, people in Judea and Galilee paid several taxes: a poll tax, land taxes, tolls on goods, and the temple tax. Tax collectors bought the right to collect tolls and could keep whatever they took above the required amount, so many grew rich by overcharging. Because they worked for the occupying power, they were seen as traitors and grouped with “sinners.”

**What it shows about God:** Jesus called Matthew, a tax collector, to follow Him and went to Zacchaeus’ house — showing that no one is beyond God’s grace.

### The temple and sacrifices

The temple in Jerusalem was the one place where sacrifices were offered to the LORD. Priests offered animals and grain for sin, thanksgiving, and fellowship, and once a year the high priest entered the Most Holy Place on the Day of Atonement. Solomon’s temple was destroyed in 586 BC; the second temple, rebuilt after the exile and greatly expanded by Herod, stood in Jesus’ day.

**What it shows about God:** The sacrifices taught that sin is serious and that God provides a way to come near. They pointed to Jesus, the final sacrifice, whose death tore the temple curtain in two.

### The festivals

Israel’s year was marked by feasts. Passover remembered the rescue from Egypt; Pentecost (the Feast of Weeks) celebrated the wheat harvest; the Feast of Tabernacles (Booths) remembered the wilderness years. Three times a year men were to go up to Jerusalem, and in Jesus’ day the city filled with pilgrims.

**What it shows about God:** The feasts retell what God has done. Jesus died at Passover as the true Lamb, and the Spirit came at Pentecost.

### The synagogue

Synagogues — local gathering places for prayer, reading the Scriptures, and teaching — probably developed after the exile and were found in towns across the Jewish world by Jesus’ day. On the Sabbath, the Law and Prophets were read, and visiting teachers might be invited to speak. Paul usually began in the synagogue when he arrived in a new city.

**What it shows about God:** In the Nazareth synagogue, Jesus read Isaiah’s promise of good news for the poor and said, “Today this Scripture has been fulfilled.”

### Pharisees, Sadducees, and other groups

In Jesus’ day, Pharisees were respected teachers devoted to keeping the law and its traditions in everyday life. Sadducees were priestly aristocrats who controlled the temple and rejected belief in the resurrection. Essenes withdrew to live in pure communities (probably including Qumran), and Zealots wanted to drive out Rome by force. Samaritans worshiped the God of Israel on Mount Gerizim and were bitterly divided from the Jews.

**What it shows about God:** Jesus confronted the pride of the religious, crossed the divide to a Samaritan woman, and called a Zealot and a tax collector into the same band of disciples.

### Life under Rome

Rome took control of Judea in 63 BC and ruled through client kings like Herod and his sons, and later through governors like Pontius Pilate. Roman soldiers kept order, and the “peace of Rome” made trade and travel easier. But taxes, foreign troops, and pagan symbols were hated, and revolts were crushed — ending in the destruction of Jerusalem in AD 70.

**What it shows about God:** Caesar Augustus ordered a census — and God used it to bring Jesus to be born in Bethlehem, as promised. The true King was born under an empire that thought it ruled the world.

### Crucifixion

Rome reserved crucifixion for slaves, rebels, and the worst criminals. It was designed to be slow, public, and humiliating — a warning to everyone passing by. Roman citizens were generally exempt. To Jews, anyone hung on a tree was under God’s curse; to Romans, the cross was too shameful to mention in polite company.

**What it shows about God:** That is how far God’s love went: Christ became a curse for us, so that we could be redeemed.

### Burial customs

The dead were buried quickly, usually the same day. Wealthier families used tombs cut into rock, closed with a large stone; bodies were wrapped in linen with spices. In Jesus’ day, after about a year, the bones were often gathered into a stone box (an ossuary) so the tomb could be reused. Mourning was loud and public, sometimes with hired mourners.

**What it shows about God:** Abraham bought a tomb in the land God promised — an act of faith. And Jesus’ tomb, sealed with a stone, could not hold Him.

### Writing, scrolls, and letters

Scriptures were copied by hand on scrolls of leather or papyrus — expensive and precious, so most people heard Scripture read aloud rather than owning it. Letters were often dictated to a trained scribe (Romans names Tertius) and carried by a trusted friend, who might read the letter aloud to the church and explain it.

**What it shows about God:** God preserved His Word through generations of careful copying — and He spoke through letters written to real churches with real problems.
