# Translation Bridge — cultural notes

These short notes appear on each key word's page under **“Across cultures.”**

**To hide a note:** in `src/lib/translationBridge/culturalNotes.ts`, change `reviewed: true` to `reviewed: false` for that word. To edit one, change its `text`.

## hesed  ·  published

Translators often need two or three words to carry hesed, because many languages separate “love,” “loyalty,” and “mercy.” In cultures with strong family or clan obligations, hesed can be explained through the faithfulness expected of close kin — the loyalty Ruth showed Naomi, and the loyalty God shows His people.

## shalom  ·  published

Many languages have a greeting that wishes well-being, not just calm — for example, the Arabic “salaam” (from the same Semitic root) and the Swahili “amani.” Translators look for words that include health, wholeness, and right relationships, since a word meaning only “no war” would lose much of shalom.

## kavod  ·  published

Where honor and shame shape daily life, “glory” connects naturally with honor and reputation. In other cultures it can sound like brightness or fame only, so translators sometimes add words like “greatness” or “majesty” to carry its sense of weight and worth.

## goel  ·  published

The kinsman-redeemer custom has close parallels in many family-based societies, where a relative is expected to pay a debt or protect a widow. Where it has no parallel, translators may describe the role (“the relative who buys back”) rather than use a single word.

## berit  ·  published

Many cultures make binding agreements with rituals — shared meals, oaths, blood rites, or exchanged gifts. Translators often choose the local word for the most solemn kind of agreement, so that “covenant” does not sound like an ordinary contract.

## lev  ·  published

Languages locate the inner life in different parts of the body. In some, feelings and thoughts live in the liver or the stomach rather than the heart. Translators may use that local idiom so that “heart” carries the same meaning of the inner self — thinking, choosing, and feeling.

## ruach  ·  published

Very few languages have one word that means spirit, breath, and wind together. Translators must choose one in each verse, which is why Genesis 1:2 can read “Spirit of God,” “wind from God,” or “breath of God” in different translations.

## yhwh  ·  published

Translations handle God’s name in different ways: “the LORD” (English), “l’Éternel” (“the Eternal,” in some French Bibles), “Jehovah” (in some older translations), or a word meaning “Lord” or “Master.” Each tries to honor the name while being understood.

## charis  ·  published

In the Roman world, a patron’s gift created an obligation to return honor and loyalty. The New Testament uses that world of gift-giving while overturning it: God’s grace is given to people who cannot repay. In gift-based cultures, this contrast can be especially striking.

## logos  ·  published

Translating John 1:1 is hard in every language. Some use the ordinary word for “word”; others use a word for “message,” “speech,” or “the one who speaks for God,” so readers understand that Jesus is God’s self-expression.

## abba  ·  published

Abba was the word children and adults used for their father within the family. Translations usually keep “Abba” and add “Father,” so that readers notice the closeness of the relationship Jesus invites us into.

## koinonia  ·  published

English “fellowship” can sound like a social gathering. In many communal cultures, koinonia is easier to grasp as sharing one life and shared resources — like an extended family that eats from the same pot.
